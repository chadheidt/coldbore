Do I start a new chronograph string for each charge (or each seating depth)?

Yes: one string per charge, or per seating depth, shot in the order of your plan. Loadscope lines the strings up with your plan in that order. Fire foulers or sighters into their own short string; Loadscope sets a short first string aside. If several charges ran together in one string, Loadscope can't split them (Confirm your range day warns that they'd all land on one charge); use Enter data by hand and type each charge's speeds instead.

Check every row, then click Confirm and import. Loadscope doesn't edit speeds after the import today; Tools, Restore From Backup takes the load back to before it.

What powder charge or seating depth should I use? Is my best load safe?

Loadscope does not recommend a charge, a seating depth or any load data. It scores the loads you actually tested and names the one that scored best. That pick is a ranking of your own loads, not a sign that a load is safe. Get start and maximum charges and lengths from current published reloading manuals, start below maximum, work up carefully and watch for pressure signs.

Does the order I shoot the charges matter? Can I go high to low, or shuffle them?

Yes, order matters. Loadscope matches each chronograph string and each target to a charge by the order you fired them, so shoot your ladder in the planned order. Plan it Low → High or High → Low with the Direction buttons. Loadscope doesn't take a shuffled order today: even with Enter my own, each charge must keep climbing (or keep falling on High → Low), and none can repeat.

How many shots per charge should I shoot? Is 3 enough?

Loadscope scores the shots you fire, even fewer than planned; Shots per string starts at 5 for a powder ladder or tuner test and 3 for a seating test. SD and ES need at least 2 shots (a charge with only one shot is not scored), and a first string with half or fewer of your usual shots is set aside as a fouler. A few shots per charge finds the promising zone; shoot about 10 at your one or two finalists, and 20 to 30 before you trust an SD. For a tuner, 5 shots a setting.

How do I plan and shoot a barrel tuner test?

Choose Barrel tuner, then on the plan screen enter Start setting, Step (marks), Number of steps and Marks per turn (how many marks make one full turn on your tuner's dial). Follow your tuner maker's instructions for how far to move it; Loadscope counts up from your start. Shoot one group per setting in the order listed, 5 shots a setting if you can, and measure the targets. Your charge and seating depth stay fixed, and speed is not part of a tuner score.

How does Loadscope pick my best load, and why isn't my tightest group the winner?

Every charge (or seating depth) is scored on four things: velocity flat spot, velocity SD, mean radius and vertical SD. Each is put on a 0-to-1 scale across the loads you shot (best = 0), multiplied by its weight, and added up. The lowest total is your best load. A load can win without coming first in any single test, and the smallest group alone does not decide it. Click Why this WON beside the winner to see how it ranked in each test.

What are the default scoring weights?

For a new load they are set from your test distance and slide smoothly between these marks (flat spot / velocity SD / mean radius / vertical SD). Why does Loadscope score mean radius instead of group size?

Group size is the distance between your two widest shots, so one flier decides it, and it tends to grow as you add shots. Mean radius is the average distance of every shot from the center of the group, so every shot counts and one flier moves it far less. Group size is still shown on Results, but it is not scored. Mean radius is measured from the group's own center, not from your aim point.

What is the velocity flat spot score, and what if my ladder has no flat spot?

Flat spot measures how little your average speed changes from one charge to the next: each charge gets the average change in fps per grain to the charges beside it, and flatter scores better. It carries 5 percent by default, because a flat spot found on three to five shots is often noise. It is never pass or fail, so every ladder still gets a winner. Why this WON may note that your shot count is too small to show clear speed changes; that never changes the winner.

What is vertical SD and why does it count so much?

Vertical SD is how spread out your shots are up and down, measured around the group's center. On a powder ladder it carries 35 percent by default at 100 yards, 25 at 500 and 20 at 1000, because the powder charge acts most directly on vertical. Sideways spread is not a separate score, but it still counts through mean radius, which measures every shot's distance from center in any direction.

Why isn't extreme spread (ES) in the score, and where can I see ES and every shot?

The score uses velocity SD, because ES depends only on your fastest and slowest shot and one odd round can double it. ES is still shown: on Results, the Every shot button above the worksheet lists each charge's shot count, average, SD, ES and every shot speed in the order you fired, however many you shot. The worksheet itself shows the first five shots. A wide ES with a tight SD usually means one odd round, not a bad load.

How does Loadscope calculate SD, and why might it differ from my chronograph?

If your chronograph file's average and SD match the shots in it, Loadscope keeps the file's numbers. If only the SD is missing, it works the SD out from the shots; if the summary no longer matches the shots (say, shots deleted in a spreadsheet), it works out the average, SD and ES fresh. It uses the sample SD (n minus 1), also for speeds typed in or edited on Confirm your range day. One exception: a Garmin file that says it left shots out of its own calculation keeps Garmin's numbers.

Is my SD good? What SD or ES should I aim for?

Loadscope gives SD no pass mark. It ranks the charges you shot against each other, and a lower SD scores better. How much SD matters depends on your distance and on how many shots it came from: an SD from three or five shots is mostly noise. The Learn article "SD and ES in Load Development: What's a Good Number?" on loadscope.app gives a rough community yardstick and the reasons behind it.

What does the line under my winner mean, "is the next closest" or "too close to call"?

Loadscope always names a winner. On a powder ladder, when the next-best charge is close enough that a re-shoot could plausibly flip the two, a line appears under the winner naming it, with how many rounds each to shoot head to head (up to 10). If you already shot enough that more rounds are unlikely to separate them, it says the two are too close to tell apart. No line usually means a clear winner, but the line needs each group's spread from your target data, so some target files never show it.

How do I read the Results page?

The gold BEST LOAD bar names your winner: the best charge on a powder ladder, or on a seating test the best depth beside your test charge. "Best in" marks any single test it topped. Powder Charges and Seating Depths hold your worksheet: Composite Score (lower is better) and a Winner column marking the winner, 2nd and 3rd. Powder Charges charts average speed (with SD bars) and SD by charge; Seating Depths charts mean radius and vertical SD by depth. Scoring holds the weights; Barrel Tuner appears after a tuner test.

What drag model does the DOPE solver use, and how accurate is it?

A point-mass solver with the standard G7 and G1 drag curves, the same kind as Strelok, GeoBallistics and the free Hornady and Berger calculators. In testing against JBM Ballistics' standard G7 solver it matched within 0.07 mil, elevation and wind, out to 1000 yards on four reference loads. Measured-drag solvers such as Applied Ballistics and Hornady 4DOF pull ahead deep in the transonic, and Loadscope doesn't take a custom drag curve today. If your bullet goes transonic inside the card's ranges, the DOPE Card says where. Confirm by live fire.

What are the lands, jump and jam, and does Loadscope find my lands?

The lands are the raised ridges of the rifling. Jump is the gap a bullet travels before it touches them; jam means it is seated out into them, which Loadscope shows as a negative jump. Loadscope doesn't measure where your lands are: find them with a tool made for it and follow your reloading manual. A seating test can then be planned in CBTO, COAL or jump off the lands, whichever way you measure.

I have a general reloading question (neck tension, where to put the chronograph, which powder). Can Loadscope answer it?

Loadscope scores the loads you test; it doesn't give reloading advice or load data. For questions like neck tension, case prep or which powder, use your reloading manuals and the component maker; for where to set up your chronograph, follow its own instructions.
