.338 Lapua Magnum Load Development: A Practical Guide

The .338 Lapua Magnum is a serious extreme-long-range cartridge — it drives heavy, high-BC .338 bullets fast enough to stay supersonic to roughly 1,500 metres with standard 250-grain Lapua loads in warm weather, and farther with the 300s. It is expensive to feed and it recoils hard, which is exactly why you want a settled, well-developed load rather than burning barrel and brass chasing one.

Safety first: everything below is a starting point, not load data. Always begin at the minimum charge in a current reloading manual for your exact bullet and powder, work up in small steps, and watch for pressure signs. Your rifle, brass, and components are not the ones in someone else’s data.

Why the .338 Lapua is worth developing carefully

Its case capacity pushes 250–300-grain bullets with some of the best ballistic coefficients made, so a good load holds velocity and bucks wind at distances most cartridges cannot reach. But components are costly and recoil punishes sloppy shooting, so every round of your ladder counts. With a barrel this expensive to replace and brass this expensive to buy, every round of the workup should be answering a question.

What shooters reach for (starting points)

  • Powder: Retumbo, H1000, US869, Vihtavuori N570 and Reloder 33 are popular .338 Lapua powders; the big case wants slow-burning magnum powders — make sure your manual lists your exact bullet and powder pair before you start.
  • Bullets: the 250–300-grain high-BC match bullets (Berger 300 OTM, Hornady 285 ELD-M, Sierra 300 MatchKing) are extreme-range staples; the higher the BC, the better a bullet holds its velocity at distance.
  • Brass: Lapua and Peterson both make .338 Lapua brass; given the cost per round, uniform well-prepped brass is worth every minute.
  • Primers: the .338 Lapua case takes a large rifle primer, and magnum primers such as the Federal 215M and CCI 250 are common with its slow powders. Use the primer your published data names, and change it only with a new ladder.

The process — same as any cartridge, .338 Lapua flavored

  1. Pick your components and look up the published min and max for that bullet/powder pair.
  2. Run a powder ladder. Load a few charges across the safe range and chronograph them to find the velocity flat-spot — the charge window where small powder changes barely move velocity. How to read a powder ladder →
  3. Run a seating-depth test at your chosen charge to tune how the bullet jumps to the lands and tighten the group. How to run a seating-depth test →
  4. Confirm at distance and record your dope, so the load proves itself where it counts — a cartridge built for extreme range has to prove itself there, so confirm your load and dope well past 1,000 yards.

Where .338 Lapua ladders go wrong

Buying 300s for a barrel cut for 250s. The .338 Lapua grew up around a 250-grain military load, and the reference Sako TRG-42 described on Wikipedia has a 1-in-12 twist. Berger lists 1-in-12 as enough for its 250-grain bullets but 1-in-10 as the minimum for its 300s. Sako now lists 1-in-10 for the TRG-42 in .338 Lapua, and 1-in-10 is the common twist for the cartridge, but a used rifle or an older barrel can be either. Read the stamp on the barrel before the 300s go on the bench.

Letting recoil shoot the hot end. The .338 Lapua kicks hard, and if you shoot a long ladder bottom to top, the top charges get shot last, by the most tired shoulder of the day. A pulled shot there looks like the charge opened up. Keep the rifle set up the same way for every rung (same brake or suppressor, same rest, same bags), take a break between strings, and check the velocity numbers before you blame the charge for a wide group.

Laddering in summer, shooting in winter. The figures Wikipedia gives for the TRG-42 with the 250-grain military load cut its maximum practical range from 1,500 metres in warm summer conditions to 1,300 at freezing and 1,100 in Arctic winter, when muzzle velocity may drop to 880 m/s. That is factory ammunition, not your handload, but the point carries over: re-chronograph the load in the cold before you trust dope you built in July.

Changing brass and keeping the charge. Lapua designed the case with a strengthened web and a hardness gradient, hard at the head and softer toward the mouth, to carry the cartridge’s pressure. Another maker’s case is a different component. If you switch, treat it as new brass and run the ladder again from the bottom.

Barrel life and the .338 Lapua round budget

One widely cited set of barrel-life numbers for this cartridge comes from Finnish Army testing, as reported on Wikipedia: its TRG-42 barrels kept their accuracy for 7,000 to 8,000 rounds of Lapua’s Lock Base factory ammunition, but heavy handloads cut that to 1,500 to 2,000 rounds through faster throat erosion. Two lessons for a workup come out of that. First, the top of the charge window is where you spend barrel fastest, so a load that sits in a velocity flat-spot below the maximum is worth more than the last few feet per second. .338 Lapua load development: common questions

What twist rate do I need for 300-grain bullets in a .338 Lapua? Berger lists 1-in-10 as the minimum for its 300-grain bullets and 1-in-12 for its 250s. 1-in-10 is the common .338 Lapua twist, but some rifles were built 1-in-12 for the 250-grain military load, so check the twist on your barrel.

Does SAAMI or C.I.P. data apply to the .338 Lapua? Both describe the same cartridge. C.I.P. registered it in 1989, and in January 2013 SAAMI chose to use the C.I.P. rulings, adding some dimensions of its own. Load from one manual and stay inside its maximums.

Will cold weather change my .338 Lapua load? It can. Published figures for the 250-grain military load show velocity and usable range falling as the temperature drops. Re-chronograph your load in the conditions you will shoot in and update your dope from that.

How long does a .338 Lapua barrel last? Finnish Army testing, as reported on Wikipedia, found 7,000 to 8,000 rounds of accurate life with Lapua factory ammunition, and 1,500 to 2,000 with heavy handloads. How hard you load is the part you control.
