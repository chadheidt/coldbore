.260 Remington Load Development: A Practical Guide

The .260 Remington helped put the 6.5mm bore into long-range precision shooting — Remington introduced it in 1997, about a decade before the Creedmoor. It is a .308 case necked down to 6.5mm, runs the same high-BC 6.5mm bullets, shoots flat, and is mild enough to stay accurate in a wide range of rifles. Some shooters still prefer it over the Creedmoor, especially in custom rifles with long throats and magazines that let it use its extra case capacity.

Safety first: everything below is a starting point, not load data. Always begin at the minimum charge in a current reloading manual for your exact bullet and powder, work up in small steps, and watch for pressure signs. Your rifle, brass, and components are not the ones in someone else’s data.

.260 Remington vs. 6.5 Creedmoor

The two cartridges are close in case capacity and produce similar velocities — the .260 holds about a grain more water. The catch is length: the .260 case is longer, so in a short-action magazine the long, high-BC bullets have to be seated deeper, into the powder space, and that gives back the capacity edge. The Creedmoor’s shorter case was designed around those long bullets. In a custom chamber with a long throat and a magazine that allows a longer round, the .260 can match or slightly exceed Creedmoor velocity with the same bullet; in a factory rifle, the two are effectively tied.

Beyond that, the Creedmoor’s advantage is commercial: more factory ammo options, more brass, and a larger data base. If you already own a .260 or are building a custom rifle and want to consider it alongside the Creedmoor, the ballistics are essentially the same and the component base is more than adequate for precision work.

What shooters reach for (starting points)

  • Powder: H4350 is one of the most popular .260 Remington precision powders, as it is for the 6.5 Creedmoor — the two cartridges run in a similar burn-rate range, and some shooters use faster powders behind lighter bullets. Winchester StaBALL 6.5 (2019) is a newer option that Hodgdon sells as temperature-insensitive. Make sure your manual lists your exact bullet and powder pair before you start.
  • Bullets: the 140-class match bullets that lead the 6.5 Creedmoor work equally well here — Hornady 140 ELD-M, Berger 140 Hybrid, Sierra 142 MatchKing, and Hornady 147 ELD-M. Longer-throated custom chambers also accommodate the 156 Berger EOL. In a factory .260 chamber, check how the longest bullets fit your throat and magazine before you buy a box.
  • Brass: Lapua .260 Remington brass is the top pick for precision work — consistent, long-lasting, and available. Nosler, Peterson, and Remington are all usable. Federal brass is common in factory ammunition and works well once prepped. The .260’s .308 parent case means brass is easy to form from .308 Win stock if needed.
  • Primers: standard large rifle — Federal 210M, CCI 200, CCI BR-2. The .260 does not need magnum primers with standard loads and H4350-class powders.

The process — same as any cartridge, .260 flavored

  1. Pick your components and find published min/max for your bullet and powder pair. Hodgdon, Alliant, and Nosler manuals all cover the .260 thoroughly; Berger and Sierra publish load data for their bullets specifically. Start at the published minimum.
  2. Run a powder ladder. Load a spread of charges across the safe range and chronograph each one. You are finding the velocity flat-spot — the charge window where velocity changes slowly with charge weight. How to read a powder ladder →
  3. Run a seating-depth test at your confirmed charge. A short factory throat means a long bullet has to sit deeper in the case to stay off the lands, so check where the lands are in your own chamber first. Measure your chamber and work within it — a bullet jammed hard into a short throat is a pressure problem, not a performance advantage. How to run a seating-depth test →
  4. Confirm at distance and record your DOPE. With the same bullet at similar velocity, the .260 at 600 yards behaves much like the Creedmoor. Verify there.

Where .260 Remington ladders go wrong

Borrowing Creedmoor data. The .260 and the 6.5 Creedmoor shoot the same bullets and often the same powders, so it is tempting to build a .260 ladder from Creedmoor numbers. They are different cases with different lengths and capacities. Use data printed for the .260 Remington with your exact bullet and powder, and start at its minimum.

Assuming a Creedmoor twist. Hornady set the Creedmoor at 1:8, but .260 rifles were never held to one rate: Ron Spomer, writing in Outdoor Life in 2020, lists 1:10, 1:9 and at least one 1:8 among the makers, and notes that the slower barrels struggle to stabilize bullets much heavier than 142 grains. Find your twist before you buy a box of the long ones.

Stepping deeper into a full case. At short-action magazine length, a long 6.5 bullet already sits down into the .260’s case body and takes up powder room. Each deeper step in a seating test takes away more of it at the same charge, so run those steps from a charge with room below the top of your ladder, and look over the brass after every one.

Mixing formed brass with .260 headstamps. A lot of .260 brass started life as another cartridge. Cases necked down from .308 or 7mm-08 come out with thicker necks than factory .260 cases, so neck tension will not match. Keep formed and factory brass in separate lots and ladder each on its own.

Making .260 brass from other cases

Lapua, Nosler, Peterson and Remington all make .260 brass, but because the cartridge is a necked-down .308 you can also make it from three common parents: .308 Winchester and 7mm-08 Remington, both necked down, or .243 Winchester, necked up. Which way you go changes the neck:

  • Necking down from .308 or 7mm-08 thickens the neck walls. Rifle Shooter’s article on forming cases says any significant squeeze to a smaller diameter does this, and the thickest may need turning. Check the neck diameter of a loaded dummy against your chamber drawing or reamer print before laddering.
  • Necking up from .243 thins the neck walls instead, so turning is not needed, but run the necks over an expander rather than forcing the step in a sizing die.

Whichever parent you use, form the whole batch the same way, from one headstamp, and keep it together through both the powder ladder and the seating test. A ladder shot on a mix of parents tells you about the brass, not the charge.

.260 Remington load development: common questions

Can I use 6.5 Creedmoor load data for a .260 Remington? No. The two cases differ in length and capacity, so use data published for the .260 Remington with your exact bullet and powder, and start at its minimum.

Is the .260 Remington the same as the 6.5-08? The .260 began as the 6.5-08 wildcat, a .308 case necked down to 6.5mm, and Remington introduced it as a factory cartridge in 1997. Published load data is listed under the .260 Remington name.

Will a .260 stabilize 140-grain bullets? That depends on your barrel’s twist, not the cartridge name. .260 rifles have been made in 1:10, 1:9 and 1:8, and Berger, for one, lists 1:8 as the minimum twist for its 140 Hybrid Target. Check the maker’s figure against your barrel before you buy.

Can I make .260 brass from .308 brass? Yes. Necking .308 down to 6.5mm thickens the neck walls, so check a loaded dummy’s neck diameter against your chamber, and keep formed cases separate from factory .260 brass during a workup.
