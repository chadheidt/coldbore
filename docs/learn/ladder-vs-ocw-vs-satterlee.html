Ladder Test vs OCW vs Satterlee: Which Load Development Method Is Best?

There is no single best load development method, because the three popular ones answer different questions. A powder ladder and the Satterlee 10-round test look at velocity over a chronograph. OCW looks at where your groups land. Here is what each one tells you, where each one misleads, and how to use them together.

The detail on each: how to read a powder ladder, the Satterlee 10-round method and the OCW method.

What is the difference between a ladder test and the Satterlee method?

The Satterlee method is a ladder with one shot per charge. That makes it fast: ten rounds and one range trip to point you at a promising charge, instead of shooting groups at a dozen charges. It also means every velocity is a single shot with all the normal round-to-round scatter, so a flat spot can appear by luck and vanish if you shot it again. A fuller ladder gives you more shots to read, but the same warning applies: a ladder gets fooled more often than most shooters expect.

How is OCW different?

OCW, developed by Dan Newberry, ignores the chronograph and reads the target. You shoot round-robin, one shot at each charge, then back to the top, so wind, mirage and barrel heat spread evenly across all the charges. You look for two or three neighboring charges that land in the same place. The goal is a forgiving charge, one least fussy about a small powder change, and not the smallest group on the day.

Which one is best?

The one that matches the question you are asking.

  • Where should I look? A ladder or the Satterlee test. Scout wide and cheap.
  • Which charge holds its point of impact? OCW, read off the target.
  • Which charge is consistent in speed? A ladder over a chronograph, read for flat spot and SD.

Velocity consistency and point-of-impact stability usually travel together, but not always, which is why plenty of shooters do both.

Why do the methods disagree?

Mostly sample size. Every number you pull off a target or a chronograph is an estimate, and estimates get more honest the more shots feed them. A few shots per charge finds the rough zone but cannot pick the winner between two close charges. Standard deviation is the worst offender: off three or five shots it is mostly noise. We ran the numbers in our flat-spot study, and how many shots per charge lays out a practical budget.

A practical order to use them

  1. Scout wide and cheap. A short ladder or a Satterlee test narrows a dozen charges down to a window. Always start low and work up, watching for pressure.
  2. Confirm your finalists. Pour the rounds into the one or two charges that looked best: about 10 shots each, and 20 to 30 before you bet a match on the numbers.
  3. Read the target too. If you like OCW, check that your finalist holds its point of impact as the charge steps up or down.
