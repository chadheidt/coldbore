How to Score a Barrel Tuner Test

A barrel tuner test is simple to shoot and hard to read. Groups get smaller, then bigger, then smaller again as you turn the dial, and on three to five shots a lot of that is noise. Here's how to score one so the setting you pick still holds next month, not just on test day.

Shooting the test

Settle your load first — a tuner fine-tunes a load that already shoots. Then shoot one group at each tuner setting, stepping across the dial in even marks, with the same load and the same aim, in order. For example, step five marks at a time across a full turn. Fire the same number of shots at every setting so the groups can be compared.

What to measure

  • Vertical — how tall the group is. Recoil tips the muzzle up and down, so vertical is the axis a tuner works on most. A group that goes tall is the clearest sign a setting is out of tune.
  • Mean radius — the average distance of each shot from the group's center. It uses every shot, so it's steadier on small groups than group size, which only counts the two worst holes. (Mean radius vs group size)
  • Width, with care. A group that's wide but short is often the wind. A group that's tall is the tune.
  • Velocity, as a check only. Turning a tuner shouldn't change your speed. If velocity wanders across the test, something else changed — the ammunition, or barrel heat — and the groups deserve less trust.

Why the smallest group is the wrong pick

On three to five shots, group size bounces around even when nothing changed. Two settings whose groups are within roughly a quarter to a third of each other can't honestly be told apart at those shot counts; the difference is inside the noise.

Tuner response isn't smooth either. A good setting sitting alone between two bad neighbors is on a steep part of the tune, and a little temperature or fouling moves it off. What you want is a run of neighboring settings that all shot well. Sit in the middle of that run and you have good ground on both sides. If your dial turns a full circle, the last mark sits next to the first, so a good run can cross zero.

Watch where each group lands, too. If the group's position jumps a long way between two neighboring settings, that setting sits on a steep part of the tune even if the group itself was small.

How Loadscope scores a tuner test

Pick Barrel tuner when you plan the ladder and enter the settings in dial marks. After you shoot, measure your targets from a photo or import them, and Loadscope:

  • scores every setting half on how tall the group is and half on mean radius, each compared against the rest of your test. That split is a judgment call, so it's shown on the Scoring tab and you can change it;
  • rules out any setting whose group is far taller than the rest, with the bar set by how many shots you fired, so a small sample doesn't throw out a good setting by chance;
  • treats settings within the noise of the best as equally good, instead of crowning a winner by a hair;
  • recommends the middle of the longest run of good neighboring settings — always a setting you actually shot;
  • flags a big jump in point of impact between neighboring settings when it knows where you were aiming.

It doesn't score velocity. A tuner doesn't change your speed, so scoring velocity would be scoring the ammunition, not the tune.

The honest limit

Published evidence on tuner position is thin, and three shots can't always tell a tall group from an unlucky one. Loadscope is built to say that plainly rather than hand you a confident number: when the settings can't be told apart, it says so. If the choice matters, shoot the middle of the run again on another day and see if it holds.
