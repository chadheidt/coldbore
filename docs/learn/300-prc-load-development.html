300 PRC Load Development: A Practical Guide

The 300 PRC was designed around one specific goal: drive long, heavy, high-BC .30 caliber bullets at real long-range velocity from a standard long action. Hornady’s own 225-grain ELD Match load is rated at 2,810 fps from a 24-inch test barrel — and that combination of weight, BC, and velocity carries a lot of energy and wind resistance a long way.

Safety first: everything below is a starting point, not load data. Always begin at the minimum charge in a current reloading manual for your exact bullet and powder, work up in small steps, and watch for pressure signs. Your rifle, brass, and components are not the ones in someone else’s data.

What the 300 PRC is built for

Many .30 caliber magnums were designed decades ago, before the era of long-ogive, high-BC match bullets. Their throats and magazine lengths can force you to seat a long, heavy bullet deep into the case — which cuts powder capacity exactly when you need it most. The 300 PRC was engineered around today’s high-BC bullets: a maximum overall length that fits standard long-action magazines, room in the throat for long bullets, and a beltless case that headspaces off the shoulder.

If you are shooting .30 caliber past 1,000 yards with premium match bullets, the 300 PRC lets the case do what modern components are capable of.

What shooters reach for (starting points)

  • Powder: slow-burning magnum powders such as H1000 and Reloder 26 are popular in the 300 PRC. For the heaviest bullets, some shooters go slower still; make sure your manual lists your exact bullet and powder pair before you start.
  • Bullets: Hornady’s factory loads use the 212-grain ELD-X and 225-grain ELD Match; the Berger 215 and 230 Hybrids are popular too. These are among the highest-BC .30 caliber bullets made.
  • Brass: Hornady, Peterson and Lapua all make 300 PRC brass. Cases need careful preparation — at this pressure level, uniform flash holes, consistent primer pocket depth, and annealed necks keep your SD numbers where they need to be.
  • Primers: large rifle magnum primers such as Federal 215M and CCI 250 are the usual choice with the big charges of slow powder; use the primer your data was developed with.

The process — same as any cartridge, 300 PRC flavored

  1. Pick your components and look up the published min and max for your bullet and powder pair. Hornady’s published data is the primary reference; Berger’s reloading data covers their bullets well. Start at the manual minimum — SAAMI lists the 300 PRC at a maximum average pressure of 65,000 psi, and pressure signs can appear quickly as you approach max.
  2. Run a powder ladder. Load a spread across the safe range and chronograph every charge. The velocity flat-spot can be narrow — precise measurement and honest data reading are not optional. How to read a powder ladder →
  3. Run a seating-depth test at your confirmed charge. The 300 PRC’s generous freebore is there for a reason — these long bullets need room. Your chamber and your bullet make the final call on how far off the lands it shoots best. How to run a seating-depth test →
  4. Confirm at distance. The 300 PRC’s case for itself is made past 1,000 yards. Record your DOPE there, not just at 100.

Where 300 PRC ladders go wrong

Sizing it like a belted magnum. The 300 Win Mag headspaces on its belt; the 300 PRC has no belt and headspaces on its shoulder. That makes shoulder position part of every round you load. Set the sizing die to move the shoulder only as far as your chamber needs, check it with a headspace gauge, and leave that setting alone for the whole ladder so case-to-case differences stay out of the velocity column.

Chasing the lands. Hornady built “deliberate chamber clearance in the throat” into the 300 PRC so long bullets are not crowded into the powder space. Depending on your chamber and magazine, a round that feeds may sit well off the lands. Plan the seating test around depths your magazine will feed instead of trying to reach the rifling.

Letting recoil into the groups. Outdoor Life rates its recoil as similar to a 300 Win Mag and calls both stout. If the last rungs of a long session are shot by a tired shooter, the groups say more about you than about the charge. Split the ladder across sessions, or use a brake or suppressor if your rifle has one.

300 PRC or 300 Win Mag: why the newer case changes the workup

The 300 Win Mag dates from 1963 and the 300 PRC from 2018, and the gap shows at the loading bench. The Win Mag is belted. The PRC is beltless, built on the .375 Ruger case with a steep 30-degree shoulder for headspacing (American Hunter). Outdoor Life points out that in a 300 Win Mag, bullets of 220 grains and up put a good part of their length below the neck-shoulder junction, eating powder space; the PRC was laid out for those bullets from the start, and Hornady recommends a 1:8 twist to stabilise its long ones.

The practical result is that a PRC lets you test the heavy, high-BC bullets it was built for without burying them in the case. The barrel you spend doing it is finite: Outdoor Life puts the effective life of a 300 PRC barrel on a high-end precision rifle at roughly 1,600 rounds, so every rung of a ladder is a real slice of it. Count the rounds before you start, not after.

300 PRC load development: common questions

Can I use 300 Win Mag brass or data in a 300 PRC? No. The 300 PRC is a different, beltless case built on the .375 Ruger, and it headspaces on the shoulder rather than a belt. Use 300 PRC brass and 300 PRC data for your exact bullet and powder.

What twist rate does a 300 PRC need? Hornady recommends a 1:8 twist for the long, heavy match bullets the cartridge was designed around. Check your bullet maker’s recommendation for the exact bullet.

How many rounds does a 300 PRC barrel last? Outdoor Life puts the effective life of a 300 PRC barrel on a high-end precision rifle at roughly 1,600 rounds, and at least 1,000 rounds on a typical hunting rifle. Your barrel, pace of fire and loads will move that, so plan the workup round count before you start.

Do I need magnum primers for the 300 PRC? Magnum primers are the usual choice with its large charges of slow powder, but use the primer listed in the data you are working from and keep it the same for the whole workup.
